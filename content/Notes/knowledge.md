What is knowledge? It is one of those questions that seems incredibly simple until you actually try to pin it down. Philosophers have been arguing about it for millennia, but we can break it down into a few core concepts that bridge the gap between abstract philosophy and everyday life.

## 1. The Classic Definition: Justified True Belief

For centuries, the gold standard definition in philosophy (rooted in Plato’s dialogues) has been that knowledge is **Justified True Belief**. To truly "know" something, three conditions must be met:

- **Belief:** You must actually believe the statement to be true.
    
- **Truth:** The statement must actually _be_ factually true. You can't genuinely "know" something that is false; you can only be mistaken about it.
    
- **Justification:** You must have good evidence, logic, or reason for your belief. A lucky guess doesn't count as knowledge.
    
## 2. Core Types of Knowledge

Knowledge is categorized by how it is stored, applied, and shared:

- **Declarative / Explicit Knowledge (*Knowing That*):** 
	- Factual information and theoretical concepts (e.g., *"Paris is the capital of France"* or *"E=mc²"*). It is easily written down and transferred.

* **Procedural Knowledge (*Knowing How*):** 
	* Skill-based and action-oriented (e.g., *how to ride a bike, write code, or perform surgery*). It focuses on practical application.

* **Tacit Knowledge (*Internalized*):** 
	* Highly personal, intuitive insights gained through experience that are incredibly difficult to verbalize or formalize (e.g., *reading the mood of a room or mastering an artistic technique*).

## 3. The DIKW Hierarchy (Data to Wisdom)
To understand knowledge, it helps to distinguish it from other cognitive states along the spectrum of data processing:

| **Stage**       | **What it is**                                                                                                          | **Example**                                                                                  |
| --------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Data**        | Raw, unorganized, and uncontextualized facts or numbers.                                                                | `38`                                                                                         |
| **Information** | Data that has been cleaned, structured, and given context.                                                              | "The patient's body temperature is 38°C (100.4°F)."                                          |
| **Knowledge**   | Information that has been processed, understood, and integrated with experience to be made actionable.                  | "A body temperature of 38°C indicates a low-grade fever; the body is fighting an infection." |
| **Wisdom**      | The ability to make good judgments, apply common sense, and discern the best course of action based on one's knowledge. |                                                                                              |

> **In short:** Information is a tool, but knowledge is the understanding of how, when, and why to use that tool.
