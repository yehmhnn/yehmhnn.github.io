### The Core Definitions

At the heart of the on/off-policy distinction are two separate mathematical policies:

- **Behavior Policy ($\pi_{\text{behavior}}$):** The policy the agent uses to actually interact with the environment, take actions, and gather data (e.g., an $\epsilon$-greedy policy that occasionally picks random actions to explore).
    
- **Target Policy ($\pi_{\text{target}}$):** The policy the agent is trying to evaluate, learn, and perfect into the absolute optimal strategy.
    

> **The Golden Rule:** 
> - An algorithm is **On-Policy** if $\pi_{\text{target}} = \pi_{\text{behavior}}$ (evaluating the policy it's actually using).
> - An algorithm is **Off-Policy** if $\pi_{\text{target}} \neq \pi_{\text{behavior}}$ (evaluating a different policy than the one it's using).

### The Off-Policy Concept: "Learning from Observing"

Off-policy learning decouples _acting_ from _learning_. Because the agent separates its exploratory behavior policy from its optimal target policy, it yields unique advantages and mechanics:

- **How it works:** The agent can learn from data generated by a completely different agent, human demonstrations, or even its own historical past experiences (replay buffers), regardless of how randomly it was acting at the time.
    
- **The Implicit Assumption:** It updates its value functions assuming it will take the _absolute best_ possible action in the future, completely ignoring whether its behavior policy actually decides to explore or make a mistake next.
    
- **Typical Example:** **[[Q-Learning]]**. It learns the value of a perfectly greedy (optimal) policy, even while the agent is still running around using an $\epsilon$-greedy strategy to explore the environment.
    

**Key Trade-off:** Off-policy methods are highly powerful because they can find the "shortest path" directly while still exploring. However, this introduces real-world risk.

> _Example ([[Cliff Walking]]):_ Q-learning will confidently learn the optimal path right along a cliff edge because it assumes perfect future play. Yet, because its _behavior policy_ still forces it to randomly explore, it will frequently trip and fall off the cliff during training.

    
| **Feature**            | **On-Policy (e.g., [[SARSA]])**                           | **Off-Policy (e.g., [[Q-Learning]])**                        |
| ---------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **What is evaluated?** | The policy currently being used ($\pi_{\text{behavior}}$) | The optimal greedy policy ($\pi_{\text{target}}$)            |
| **Exploration Factor** | Included directly in the evaluation                       | Separated completely from the evaluation                     |
| **Primary Advantage**  | **Safer.** Accounts for realistic, imperfect behavior.    | **Optimizing.** Learns the absolute optimal target directly. |
### Mathematical Comparison

**SARSA (On-Policy) Update Formula:**
$$Q(S_t, A_t) \leftarrow Q(S_t, A_t) + \alpha \left[ R_{t+1} + \gamma Q(S_{t+1}, A_{t+1}) - Q(S_t, A_t) \right]$$
* **Why it's On-Policy:** The next action $A_{t+1}$ is sampled directly from the **behavior policy** (the same exploratory policy generating the transitions). SARSA accounts for the risk of its actual exploratory behavior.

**Q-Learning (Off-Policy) Update Formula:**
$$Q(S_t, A_t) \leftarrow Q(S_t, A_t) + \alpha \left[ R_{t+1} + \gamma \max_{a} Q(S_{t+1}, a) - Q(S_t, A_t) \right]$$
* **Why it's Off-Policy:** The $\max_{a}$ operator mathematically defines a **target policy** ($\pi_{\text{target}}$) that is 100% greedy:
    $$\mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi_{\text{target}}} [Q(S_{t+1}, a)] = \max_{a} Q(S_{t+1}, a)$$
    Since this target policy completely ignores the exploratory steps taken by the **behavior policy** ($\pi_{\text{behavior}}$), the learning process is off-policy.
